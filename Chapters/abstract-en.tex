This dissertation aims to improve user navigation in \glsfirst{VR} when constrained by small physical spaces, by addressing the limitations 
of traditional \glsfirst{RDW} techniques based on portals. 
Three new alternative portal variants for \gls{VR} locomotion were developed: Movable Portals, Interactive Doors and Revolving Doors - 
contributing to the research of immersive \gls{VR} navigation in restricted physical spaces using Non-Euclidean virtual environments.

\gls{VR} applications grant users the ability to navigate large virtual environments. However, the \glsfirst{UX} is often constrained by 
the limited physical space typically available to \gls{VR} users (2.4m~x~2.2m) to move.
To address this limitation, various \gls{VR} locomotion techniques have been proposed. Controllers provide 
a simple solution to this problem, by allowing users to navigate virtual worlds while standing still.
However, controller-based approaches differ significantly from natural walking in the real world and have been shown to limit the sense of immersion.

\gls{RDW} techniques reliant on self-overlapping Non-Euclidean spaces created by portals allow users to traverse large virtual environments 
through natural walking, whilst in a limited physical space. 
However, the use of portals in \gls{VR} navigation still presents design challenges.
Placing portals near the boundaries of a virtual environment conserves physical space but limits the observable preview of the portal's 
destination. In contrast, positioning portals further from boundaries can result in unnatural, floating portals. These trade-offs create a 
design tension between maximizing navigable space and preserving the coherence and naturalness of the environment.

To address these challenges,
four different \gls{VR} locomotion techniques using portals were designed, implemented and studied, with them being:
(1) Traditional Portals - a baseline condition representing the common portals found in \gls{VR} applications and research; 
(2) Movable Portals - portals initially set on a wall with a continuous preview that users can reposition; 
(3) Interactive Doors - door-shaped portals embedded in a wall that opens interactively as a real door;
(4) Revolving Doors - portals offering a continuous transition between rooms through a circular motion.

The techniques were evaluated through a user study (N=31), to evaluate these four portal types, focusing on usability, spatial
understanding, immersion, and perceived naturalness. Results suggest that the alternative designs addressed the portal limitations 
successfully and helped convey a continuous sense of space, with each portal design revealing their strong suits.
